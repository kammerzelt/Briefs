FDA is actively enforcing against exosome products — estheticians offering them as add-ons face direct legal exposure
The FDA issued warning letters to Kimera Labs (2023), Chara Biologics (early 2025 — "CharaExo" amniotic fluid-derived exosome product), and Evolutionary Biologics (late 2024 — "EXO RNA™"). In every case the finding is the same: exosome products meet the definition of a drug and biological product requiring premarket review. No exosome product has completed full FDA drug approval as of early 2026. Estheticians and spa owners who have added exosome facials or topical exosome treatments as premium service upgrades may be operating with unapproved biologics — regardless of what the supplier claims on the label. The enforcement trend is accelerating, not pausing.
Editorial angle
A clear, Skin Inc.-specific explainer: what the FDA enforcement means for estheticians, how to evaluate supplier claims, what the difference is between a compliant and a non-compliant exosome product, and what to do if you're currently using one. This is precisely the kind of risk-mitigation content Skin Inc. is uniquely positioned to provide — and a topic where absence of guidance puts readers at legal risk.
Audience signal
Every esthetician who has added exosomes as a facial upgrade in the last two years needs this. The FDA enforcement timeline suggests urgency is not hypothetical.

Scope of practice legislation for estheticians is actively moving in multiple states — in both directions
Multiple active state bills are expanding or restricting what licensed estheticians can do. Bills allowing estheticians to perform microneedling are progressing in some states. Master esthetician scope of practice expansion bills have been signed in others. New laser hair removal licensing categories are being created. Simultaneously, bills prohibiting estheticians from performing medical treatments are advancing in states where the medspa regulatory debate is intensifying. Skin Inc.'s sitemap has /business/regulatory as a section but scope of practice legislation is a recurring, fast-moving topic that typically receives episodic coverage rather than systematic tracking.
Editorial angle
A monthly or quarterly scope of practice legislative tracker — which states are expanding, which are restricting, and what it means practically — is the kind of structured recurring content that makes Skin Inc. a must-read reference for practicing estheticians navigating a changing regulatory landscape.
Audience signal
An esthetician in a state where the microneedling bill just passed needs to know. An esthetician in a state where a restriction bill is advancing needs to know. Both are in the same readership.

safety data 2026
The medspa safety crisis is a positioning opportunity for credentialed, compliant estheticians — and Skin Inc. hasn't framed it that way yet
An estimated 20,000+ medspas are operating across the US with minimal oversight. Pennsylvania Patient Safety Authority reported 11.4 incidents per 1,000 surgical encounters in 2024 — a 9.5% year-over-year increase. FDA enforcement against illegal injectable supply chains increased throughout 2025. In 2023 a patient died after an IV infusion at a medspa whose supervising physician was over 100 miles away. As patient sophistication grows and regulatory pressure increases, the licensed, trained, compliant esthetician is positioned as the trustworthy alternative in a market full of unregulated actors. Skin Inc. covers medspa and regulatory topics but typically from a compliance-obligation angle, not from a competitive positioning angle.
Editorial angle
The trust advantage: how licensed estheticians can communicate their credentials, training, and compliance as a competitive differentiator in a market where patients are increasingly aware of safety risks. Marketing, consultation language, and professional signaling — practical for every reader.
Audience signal
Shifts from "what estheticians must do to stay compliant" to "what estheticians can gain from being compliant." Different frame, more motivating read.
